Inherited a House in Onslow County? What to Do in the First 30 Days

By Josh Eudy·October 7, 2026·Inherited Property

You did not ask for a house. Now you have one, plus a lawn, a power bill and a family group text. Here is the order I would handle it in.

The short answer

Secure the house and call the insurance company first. Then open the estate with the Clerk of Superior Court in Onslow County so someone has legal authority to sign. In North Carolina, selling within about two years of the death usually needs the personal representative involved so title can be insured. Once you know who can sign, decide whether to keep, rent, list or sell it as is.

A lot of the houses I buy and list come to people this way. Grandma passes, or a parent, and suddenly someone is the family member in charge of a property they did not plan for. It is a hard time to be making real estate decisions, so here is a calm order of operations. None of this is legal advice. An estate attorney is worth the call, and I am happy to point you to one.

1. Secure the house

  • Call the insurance company. Tell them the owner has passed and the house may be vacant. Many policies change or limit coverage on a vacant home, and you want to know before something happens, not after.
  • Keep the utilities on. On the coast, a closed-up house with no power and no air conditioning grows mold fast. A few months of power bills is cheap compared to a mold problem.
  • Lock it up and check on it. Pick up the mail, cut the grass, and make it look lived in.
  • Do not clear it out yet. Until it is clear who has authority, avoid giving away or selling what is inside.

2. Who has the authority to sell an inherited house in North Carolina?

In North Carolina, an estate is handled through the Clerk of Superior Court in the county where the person lived. For Onslow County, that is the Estates Division of the Clerk of Superior Court at the courthouse, 625 Court Street in Jacksonville. Call ahead for an appointment.

  • If there is a will, the executor named in it applies to the Clerk and receives letters testamentary.
  • If there is no will, a family member applies to be the administrator and receives letters of administration.

That person, the personal representative, is usually the one who signs for the estate.

3. Can you sell an inherited house before the estate is settled?

In North Carolina, real estate generally passes to the heirs when someone dies. But there is a catch: for roughly the first two years, a sale usually needs the personal representative involved so the buyer's title insurance can cover it. Your closing attorney will tell you exactly what is needed for your situation. If there are several heirs, expect that all of them will need to agree and sign.

4. Find the mortgage, the taxes and any liens

  • Keep the mortgage current if you can, and call the servicer to let them know.
  • Reverse mortgages move fast. They generally come due after the borrower passes, and the servicer will send deadlines. Call them early.
  • Check the property taxes with the county tax office so nothing goes delinquent.
  • Look for liens or old debts tied to the house. Your attorney or a title search will turn these up.

What if the inherited home is a mobile home?

Mobile homes trip up a lot of families, because in North Carolina they are not always real estate. A mobile home that still has a DMV title is generally treated as personal property, a lot like a car, and it transfers through that title. If the title was cancelled when the home was permanently attached to the land, it is usually treated as part of the real estate. Find out which one you have early. Look for the title, and if the title still lists a co-owner who has passed, tell your attorney right away, because that adds steps.

5. Decide what you actually want

There are really four options, and the right one depends on the house and the family:

  • Keep it and move in.
  • Rent it out. Good for some houses, a headache for others, especially if the heirs live out of town. Here is how renting it out works.
  • Fix it up and list it. Usually the highest price, but it takes money, time and someone local to manage it.
  • Sell it as is. No repairs, no clean-out, and the family is done. That is what my cash offer is for.

6. Get a real number, not a website estimate

Online estimates have never been inside the house, and inherited homes are often the ones they miss on most. I will walk it and give you two numbers: what it would likely sell for if we listed it, and what I would pay for it as is. Then the family can decide with real information instead of a guess.

This is general information, not legal advice. Estate rules depend on the facts, so talk to a North Carolina estate attorney before you sign anything.
