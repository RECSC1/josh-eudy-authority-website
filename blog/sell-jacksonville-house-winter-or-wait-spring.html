Should You Sell Your Jacksonville House This Winter, or Wait Until Spring?

By Josh Eudy·November 18, 2026·Selling

Spring is the famous selling season. Everybody knows it, and most of the advice online says wait for it. But that advice was not written for a Marine Corps town, and it was not written for the market Onslow County is in right now. Here is the honest version.

The short answer

If your house is ready to show and your life says go, winter is usually the better move here, not the worse one. Jacksonville runs on orders, not on the school calendar. Buyers reporting to Camp Lejeune and MCAS New River in January and February are shopping right now, while most sellers sit on their hands until April. That means less competition for more serious buyers. Wait for spring only if the house genuinely is not ready, or you want the extra months to fix it.

Every November I get the same question from sellers. Should I list now, or ride it out and put it on the market in the spring when everybody says there are more buyers? It is a fair question. The problem is that the standard answer, the one you will read on every national real estate blog, is built on a picture of the country that does not look much like Jacksonville. Snow. Holiday slowdowns. Families who only move in June so the kids finish the school year. None of that is wrong somewhere. It is just mostly wrong here.

So let me give you the local version, with real numbers, and then tell you the handful of cases where waiting actually is the smarter call.

What is the Onslow County market actually doing right now?

Start with where we are, because that changes the whole conversation. Redfin's data for the three months ending October 2026 puts the Onslow County median sale price at about $318,900, up 6.1 percent from the same stretch last year. So prices are holding, even rising a little. That is the good news for a seller.

Here is the part that matters more. The median home took 71 days to sell, up from 35 days a year ago. That is not a typo. Homes are sitting about twice as long as they were last fall. And 301 homes sold in October, down from 374 the year before, close to a 20 percent drop in volume. Fewer buyers, moving slower.

What that tells you is simple. This is not a market where you throw a sign in the yard and field three offers by Sunday. Price and condition decide everything now, and every extra week on the market is another week you are paying for the house while it sells. That reality does not care what season it is. It is true in April and it is true in December. Which is exactly why the season is the wrong thing to be anchored on.

Why the spring rule does not fit a military town

The whole case for waiting until spring rests on one idea: more buyers show up when it warms up and the school year ends. In most of the country, that is real. Families time the move around summer break.

Jacksonville does not run on summer break. It runs on orders. A Marine does not get to tell the Corps he would rather report in June. People PCS in here year round, and a steady share of them are reporting in the dead of winter with a date they cannot move. Those are not tire kickers waiting for the daffodils. Those are buyers who need a house by a fixed day, who are often buying from three states away off a video tour, and who are shopping in January because January is when their life told them to.

So the thing that supposedly makes winter a bad time to sell, fewer casual buyers, barely applies to the buyer who actually drives this market. If you want the longer version of how that relocation math works from the buyer's side, I wrote it out here: PCSing to Camp Lejeune, buy or rent.

The hidden cost of waiting until spring

Say you decide to wait. You are not pressing pause for free. From now until an April listing is four to five months, and during every one of them you are still paying the mortgage, the taxes, the insurance, the utilities and the lawn. On a typical Onslow County house that adds up to real money, often more than the "spring premium" people are chasing.

Then there is what you list into. Spring is when every other seller who waited finally puts their house up too. You stop competing against the thin winter inventory and start competing against the flood. In a market already running 71 days to sell, more competition is the last thing a seller needs. Winter is the season with the fewest houses on the market and, in this town, a meaningful slice of the most motivated buyers. That is the quiet advantage almost nobody uses, because they are all waiting for April.

And that is before you factor in the things nobody can promise either direction. Rates move. Inventory moves. The house that would sell well this winter is a known quantity. Next spring is a guess.

When is waiting until spring actually the right call?

I am not going to pretend winter wins every time. It does not. Here is when I tell a seller to hold:

  • The house is not ready. If it needs real repairs or updates to show well, listing it half done in any season just invites lowball offers and long days on market. Use the winter to get it right. What to fix and what to skip is its own conversation, and I will give you a punch list with real numbers before you spend a dollar. That is part of how I work a listing.
  • Your curb appeal lives outside. If the best thing about your property is the yard, the garden or the view through the trees, and right now it is brown and bare, spring genuinely shows it better. That is a fair reason to wait, as long as you are honest that it is the reason.
  • You have zero timeline pressure. No move forcing it, no carrying costs that sting, no reason you need it done. If waiting truly costs you nothing, then it is a preference, and preferences are allowed.

Notice what is not on that list: "because it is winter." The season by itself is not a reason. Your house and your life are.

If you do list this winter, what actually matters

Selling in a slower, longer market is about getting three things right, and none of them are seasonal:

  • Price it to today, not to last spring. The number that would have worked in a 35 day market will sit in a 71 day market. I price block by block on what is actually closing now, not on what your neighbor listed at and then cut twice.
  • Show it well in less daylight. It gets dark early, so professional photography and lighting matter more, not less. The house needs to look warm and lived in, every light on, heat set so buyers linger instead of rushing back to the car.
  • Make it easy to see. The serious winter buyer is often on a tight trip or buying remotely. Flexible showings, video, and fast answers win those deals. A house that is hard to get into loses to one that is not.
